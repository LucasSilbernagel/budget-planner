Most people who budget do it in a spreadsheet or on paper. In Debt.com's 2026 survey of
1,051 respondents, 27.4% of budgeters used a spreadsheet and only 21.7% used an app. The
spreadsheet is the incumbent, and for good reason: it is free, it is yours, and it bends to
any household.

It also fails in a predictable way. In the same survey, the most common reason non-budgeters
gave for not budgeting was that it is "too time-consuming" (34.2%). Spreadsheet
forums are full of the mechanics behind that: a formula typed over by accident, a `#REF!`
that spreads down a column, a header row that moved, and finally a fresh file every January
because the old one stopped adding up. Tiller, a company that sells spreadsheet budgeting,
puts setup at 30 to 60 minutes against 15 to 20 for an app and calls manual upkeep the
spreadsheet's main drawback.

Longhand Budget is the part of that spreadsheet you would keep if you could: the recurring
figures, converted to one monthly basis, with net worth, debts and savings goals modelled
rather than improvised. It is not the transactions tab. Nothing you buy is recorded, no bank
is connected, and there is no import. The free tier runs entirely in your browser. The
premium tier adds saved what-if forecasts, a PDF summary, custom profiles and categories, and
sync that stores your plan in Germany with an EU host.
